Methods and caveats

Oregon Wind Footprint Explorer

← Back to map

This research prototype turns a box drawn on the map into a summary of what is known about that ocean space from public data. Every number carries a label:

  • measured computed directly from mapped data (area, depth, distance);
  • modelled · relative from NOAA's suitability model, meaningful only relative to other cells;
  • context describes nearby ports and counties, not what happens inside the box;
  • scenario depends on an assumption you can change.

Where the model data comes from

NOAA's National Centers for Coastal Ocean Science (NCCOS) built a relative suitability model for BOEM's 2022 Oregon Call Areas, on a grid of 10-acre hexagons. It combines 30 data layers in four submodels (industry & operations, natural resources, fisheries, wind) plus hard constraints (Department of Defense exclusion area and Coast Guard shipping fairways), using a geometric mean. BOEM used it to draw the 2024 Coos Bay and Brookings Wind Energy Areas, which were rescinded in July 2025.

Citation: Carlton J. et al. (2025). NCCOS Assessment: A Wind Energy Area Siting Analysis for the Oregon Call Areas, NCEI Accession 0306723. doi:10.25921/hwtb-xm63. Report: BOEM 2024-015.

Important limits

  • Coverage: only the 2022 Call Areas off Coos Bay/Florence and Brookings/Gold Beach. A box drawn elsewhere has no model data.
  • Relative scores: a score of 0.5 can be "High" in one submodel and "Low" in another. Categories are quantiles within each submodel. Compare category mixes, not raw scores across submodels.
  • Not for regulatory, legal or navigational use (NOAA's own use limitation).

How each panel is computed

Footprint

A cell counts as inside the box if its centre falls inside. Modelled area = cells × 10 acres. Depth is ETOPO1 (1 arc-minute) interpolated to each cell. Distances are straight-line, from the cell centre to the Census coastline or to the port location.

Fishing grounds

NCCOS's fisheries submodel ranks each cell by importance to nine fisheries using effort and revenue data from NOAA Fisheries and ODFW. Cells in its lowest suitability class are the most important fishing grounds. The headline is the share of all such cells in both Call Areas that fall inside your box. This is a relative index. Translating it into dollars needs per-fishery revenue maps, which aren't public yet.

Ports nearby

For each cell, the nearest of 12 Oregon ports by straight line. Port groups follow ODFW's landing statistics. Revenue is the 2024 ex-vessel value ODFW reports for that port group, from all fishing areas. It shows how much the port depends on each fishery, not how much is caught inside the box. Port locations are approximate.

County economies

U.S. Census County Business Patterns 2023 (businesses with employees) and Nonemployer Statistics 2023 (self-employed, which includes most fishing operations). Census noise and suppression flags are carried through as published.

Capacity

Unconstrained modelled area × a power density you choose (2–6 MW/km²; 3 is a common planning value). Homes powered assumes a 45% capacity factor and 10.5 MWh per home per year. It's illustrative, not a project design.

Jobs and spending

NREL's 2016 study for BOEM modelled Oregon's economy under two floating-wind build-outs using its JEDI input-output model: 2.9 GW by 2050 with a smaller in-state supply chain, and 5.5 GW by 2050 with a larger one. Each was run with low and high "local content" (how much equipment, labour and services are bought in Oregon), which gives the low–high ranges shown.

The dashboard divides each published total by that scenario's megawatts and multiplies by your capacity:

  • Construction job-years: 4.5–7.2 per MW (2.9 GW case) or 8.0–12.0 per MW (5.5 GW case). A job-year is one full-time job for one year; "jobs per year" spreads them over an assumed 3-year build.
  • Construction GDP: $0.46–0.71M per MW or $0.84–1.04M per MW.
  • Ongoing operations jobs: 0.33–0.48 per MW or 0.42–0.60 per MW, for the life of the project (~25 years).
  • Operations GDP: $34–48K or $45–62K per MW per year.

Counts include direct (on-site), supply-chain and induced jobs (spending by those workers). All dollars are 2014 dollars, as published. Limits: scaling in proportion to MW ignores economies of scale and assumes the scenario's supply chain exists; a single small or first project would likely buy less in Oregon than either case. Results are statewide, not coastal. Costs to existing users (for example, fishing) are not subtracted, so these are gross, not net, impacts.

Source: Jimenez T, Keyser D, Tegen S, Speer B (2016). Floating Offshore Wind in Oregon: Potential for Jobs and Economic Impacts from Two Future Scenarios. NREL/TP-5000-65421; OCS Study BOEM 2016-030. Executive summary and Tables 4-1 to 4-4.

Data used

  • NOAA NCCOS Oregon WEA siting analysis, NCEI 0306723 (CC0)
  • ODFW Commercial Landing Statistics, 2024 final, by port group
  • NOAA Fisheries FOSS commercial landings, Oregon 2015–2024
  • U.S. Census CBP 2023, Nonemployer Statistics 2023, cartographic boundaries 2023
  • NREL (2016) Oregon floating offshore wind jobs study, OCS Study BOEM 2016-030
  • ETOPO1 via NOAA CoastWatch ERDDAP
  • MarineCadastre submarine cables
  • Basemap: Esri, GEBCO, NOAA, National Geographic, Garmin, HERE and contributors

Only public, aggregated data is used. Nothing here identifies a vessel, permit holder or business.

Policy context

Oregon's Offshore Wind Energy Roadmap (DLCD, under HB 4080) was finalized in July 2026 and goes to the 2027 Legislature. It describes four paths: no offshore wind, full-scale development, economic participation without hosting projects, or a pilot project. This tool doesn't favour any of them.
